Unit 1Normal Distribution

Book: Statistics for Management (BA4101, Anna University MBA Sem I, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

The Normal Distribution is the most important continuous probability distribution in statistics, underlying much of sampling theory and hypothesis testing covered in later units. This topic covers its definition, properties, and the Standard Normal Distribution.

2. Detailed Notes

6.1 Definition and Probability Function

The Normal Distribution is a continuous probability distribution that is symmetric and bell-shaped, fully described by its mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ). Its probability density function is:

f(x) = [1 ÷ (σ√(2π))] × e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)

6.2 Assumptions of the Normal Distribution

  • The underlying variable is continuous.
  • The distribution is symmetric about the mean.
  • The mean, median and mode of the distribution all coincide at the centre.

6.3 Characteristics of the Normal Distribution

  • Bell-Shaped and Symmetric: The curve is a symmetric bell shape centred at the mean.
  • Mean = Median = Mode: All three measures of central tendency are equal and located at the centre of the curve.
  • Asymptotic Tails: The curve approaches, but never quite touches, the horizontal axis as it extends in either direction.
  • Total Area = 1: The total area under the curve equals 1, representing all possible outcomes.
  • Empirical Rule: Approximately 68% of values lie within ±1σ of the mean, 95% within ±2σ, and 99.7% within ±3σ.

6.4 Standard Normal Distribution

The Standard Normal Distribution is a special case of the Normal Distribution with mean μ = 0 and standard deviation σ = 1. Any normal random variable X can be converted into the standard normal variable Z using the Z-score formula:

Z = (X − μ) ÷ σ

This conversion lets any normal distribution problem be solved using a single standard normal probability table, regardless of the original mean and standard deviation.

6.5 Characteristics of the Standard Normal Curve

  • Centred at Z = 0, with a standard deviation of 1.
  • Symmetric about Z = 0, so P(Z < 0) = P(Z > 0) = 0.5.
  • The area under the entire curve equals 1, and probabilities for any range of Z can be read directly from standard normal tables.

Why the Normal Distribution Matters

The Normal Distribution underlies the Central Limit Theorem, sampling distributions, confidence intervals, and most parametric hypothesis tests covered in the following units — making it the foundation on which the rest of inferential statistics in this course is built.

3. Key Terms

Normal Distribution
A continuous, symmetric, bell-shaped probability distribution fully described by its mean and standard deviation.
Standard Normal Distribution
A special case of the Normal Distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1.
Z-Score
The number of standard deviations a value lies from the mean, calculated as Z = (X - mu) / sigma.
Empirical Rule
The rule that approximately 68%, 95% and 99.7% of normally distributed values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean respectively.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Normal distribution: continuous, symmetric, bell-shaped; Mean = Median = Mode; total area under curve = 1.
  • Empirical rule: ~68% within +/-1 sigma, ~95% within +/-2 sigma, ~99.7% within +/-3 sigma.
  • Standard Normal Distribution: mean = 0, standard deviation = 1; Z = (X - mu) / sigma converts any normal variable to standard normal.
  • Normal distribution underlies sampling theory, confidence intervals, and parametric hypothesis tests in later units.
