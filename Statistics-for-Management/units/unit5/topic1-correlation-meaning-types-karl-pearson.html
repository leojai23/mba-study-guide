Unit 5Correlation: Meaning, Types and Karl Pearson's Coefficient

Book: Statistics for Management (BA4101, Anna University MBA Sem I, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

This closing unit moves from testing hypotheses about single variables to studying the relationship between two variables. This opening topic covers what correlation means, its types, and the most widely used measure of it: Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient.

2. Detailed Notes

1.1 Meaning of Correlation

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two numerical variables — whether, and how closely, they tend to move together.

1.2 Types of Correlation

BasisTypes
DirectionPositive Correlation (both variables increase or decrease together) vs Negative Correlation (one increases as the other decreases).
Number of VariablesSimple Correlation (between two variables) vs Multiple Correlation (one variable and two or more others considered jointly).
Nature of RelationshipLinear Correlation (the relationship approximates a straight line) vs Non-Linear (Curvilinear) Correlation (the relationship follows a curve).

1.3 Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Correlation

Karl Pearson's coefficient, r, is the most widely used measure of linear correlation between two variables X and Y:

r = Σ(X−X̄)(Y−Ȳ) ÷ √[Σ(X−X̄)² × Σ(Y−Ȳ)²]

An equivalent, computationally convenient form using raw sums is:

r = [nΣXY − (ΣX)(ΣY)] ÷ √{[nΣX² − (ΣX)²][nΣY² − (ΣY)²]}

1.4 Properties of r

  • r always lies between −1 and +1 (inclusive): −1 ≤ r ≤ +1.
  • r = +1 indicates perfect positive linear correlation.
  • r = −1 indicates perfect negative linear correlation.
  • r = 0 indicates no linear correlation (the variables may still be related non-linearly).
  • r is independent of the units of measurement of X and Y.

1.5 Interpreting the Strength of r

Value of |r|Strength
0.00 – 0.19Very weak
0.20 – 0.39Weak
0.40 – 0.69Moderate
0.70 – 0.89Strong
0.90 – 1.00Very strong

1.6 Important Caveat: Correlation Is Not Causation

A high correlation between two variables does not prove that one causes the other — the relationship could be coincidental, or both variables could be driven by a third, unmeasured factor. Correlation should always be interpreted carefully, and ideally alongside domain knowledge about the variables in question.

3. Key Terms

Correlation
A measure of the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two numerical variables.
Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Correlation (r)
The most widely used measure of linear correlation, ranging from -1 to +1.
Positive Correlation
A relationship in which both variables tend to increase or decrease together.
Negative Correlation
A relationship in which one variable tends to increase as the other decreases.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Correlation = strength and direction of linear relationship between two variables.
  • Types: Positive/Negative (direction); Simple/Multiple (number of variables); Linear/Non-Linear (nature of relationship).
  • Karl Pearson's r = Sum[(X-Xbar)(Y-Ybar)] / sqrt{Sum(X-Xbar)^2 * Sum(Y-Ybar)^2}; range: -1 <= r <= +1.
  • r = +1 perfect positive; r = -1 perfect negative; r = 0 no linear correlation.
  • Correlation does not imply causation.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Define correlation. Explain its types based on direction.
    Show model answer
    Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two numerical variables. Based on direction, it is Positive (both variables increase or decrease together) or Negative (one increases as the other decreases).
  2. State Karl Pearson's coefficient of correlation formula and its properties.
    Show model answer
    r = Sum[(X-Xbar)(Y-Ybar)] / sqrt{Sum(X-Xbar)^2 x Sum(Y-Ybar)^2}. Its properties: r always lies between -1 and +1; r = +1 is perfect positive correlation, r = -1 is perfect negative correlation, r = 0 indicates no linear correlation; and r is independent of the units of measurement.
  3. Why is it said that 'correlation does not imply causation'?
    Show model answer
    A high correlation between two variables shows they tend to move together, but does not prove one causes the other — the relationship could be coincidental, or both variables could be driven by a third, unmeasured factor, so correlation must be interpreted carefully alongside domain knowledge.
