Unit 5Coefficient of Determination and Rank Correlation

Book: Statistics for Management (BA4101, Anna University MBA Sem I, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

This topic covers two further correlation concepts: the coefficient of determination, which gives correlation a direct, intuitive interpretation, and rank correlation, used when data is ordinal rather than precisely numerical.

2. Detailed Notes

2.1 Coefficient of Determination

The coefficient of determination, r², is simply the square of Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient. It gives a directly meaningful interpretation: the proportion of the total variation in one variable that is "explained by," or associated with, its linear relationship with the other variable.

r² = (Karl Pearson's r)²

For example, if r = 0.8, then r² = 0.64, meaning 64% of the variation in one variable is associated with its linear relationship to the other variable (and the remaining 36% is associated with other factors).

Why r² Is Useful

Because r² is always between 0 and 1 regardless of the sign of r, it gives a simpler, more intuitive sense of the strength of a relationship than r itself, and is the measure most commonly reported alongside, or instead of, regression results in the next topics.

2.2 Rank Correlation (Spearman's Coefficient)

Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient measures the strength and direction of association between two variables when the data is ranked (ordinal) rather than measured on a precise numerical scale, or when the relationship is monotonic but not necessarily linear.

ρ = 1 − [6Σd² ÷ n(n²−1)]

where d is the difference between the ranks assigned to each pair of observations (d = Rank of X − Rank of Y), and n is the number of paired observations.

Procedure

  1. Rank the X values from 1 to n (and separately, rank the Y values from 1 to n).
  2. Calculate d for each pair, and then d².
  3. Sum d² across all pairs and substitute into the formula above.

Handling Tied Ranks

When two or more values are tied, each is given the average of the ranks they would otherwise occupy, consistent with the tie-handling rule used in the Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests in Unit 4.

2.3 Properties of Spearman's ρ

  • ρ also ranges from −1 to +1, with the same interpretation of direction and strength as Karl Pearson's r.
  • It requires no assumption about the underlying distribution of the data, making it a non-parametric measure of association.
  • It is especially appropriate when data is already in ranked form (e.g. rankings by two different judges).

2.4 Karl Pearson's r vs Spearman's ρ

BasisKarl Pearson's rSpearman's ρ
Data requiredInterval/ratio numerical dataOrdinal (ranked) data, or numerical data converted to ranks
Relationship detectedLinear relationships onlyAny monotonic relationship (linear or not)
Sensitivity to outliersMore sensitiveLess sensitive, since it uses ranks rather than raw values

3. Key Terms

Coefficient of Determination (r-squared)
The square of Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient, giving the proportion of variation in one variable associated with its linear relationship to another.
Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient (rho)
A non-parametric measure of association between two variables based on the ranks of the data rather than their raw values.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • r-squared (coefficient of determination) = (Karl Pearson's r)^2; proportion of variation in one variable associated with the other; always between 0 and 1.
  • Spearman's rho = 1 - [6*Sum(d^2) / n(n^2-1)], where d = difference between paired ranks.
  • Tied ranks: use average of the ranks they'd occupy.
  • Pearson's r: linear relationships, numerical data, sensitive to outliers; Spearman's rho: any monotonic relationship, ranked data, non-parametric, less sensitive to outliers.

5. Practice Questions

  1. What is the coefficient of determination? How is it calculated and interpreted?
    Show model answer
    The coefficient of determination, r-squared, is the square of Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient. It represents the proportion of the total variation in one variable that is associated with its linear relationship to the other variable; for example, r-squared = 0.64 means 64% of the variation is explained by the relationship.
  2. State the formula for Spearman's Rank Correlation Coefficient and explain its terms.
    Show model answer
    rho = 1 - [6 x Sum(d^2) / n(n^2-1)], where d is the difference between the ranks assigned to X and Y for each pair of observations, and n is the number of paired observations.
  3. Differentiate between Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.
    Show model answer
    Karl Pearson's r requires interval/ratio numerical data and detects only linear relationships, and is more sensitive to outliers. Spearman's rho works with ordinal/ranked data (or numerical data converted to ranks), detects any monotonic relationship, is non-parametric, and is less sensitive to outliers since it uses ranks rather than raw values.
