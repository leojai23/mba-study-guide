Case Studies

1. Capital Adequacy Ratio (Solved)

Scenario: A bank's Tier I and Tier II capital, along with its risk-weighted assets (on-balance-sheet and off-balance-sheet), are given. The task is to compute the bank's Capital Adequacy Ratio and assess it against the regulatory minimum.

Best-effort figures from the book: Tier I Capital ≈ ₹51.37 (lakh/crore, unit as per the book), Tier II Capital ≈ ₹4.17, Total Capital ≈ ₹55.51; Risk-Weighted Assets (on-balance-sheet) ≈ ₹699.13, plus off-balance-sheet exposure ≈ ₹19.38, giving Total RWA ≈ ₹718.5. CAR = Total Capital ÷ Total RWA ≈ 55.51 / 718.5 ≈ 7.7%.

Conclusion tested: Since RBI mandates a minimum CAR of 9% for Indian scheduled commercial banks (8% under Basel III globally), a bank computing to ~7.7% would be judged under-capitalised and required to raise additional capital.

Method covered in: Unit 2 › Capital Adequacy & Deposits

2. Asset-Liability Management (Solved, discussion-based)

Scenario: The case discusses how integrated asset-liability management has evolved into a core management discipline for banks, requiring both a technical and organisational perspective to manage risk exposures raised by the mismatch of assets and liabilities.

Questions asked: Explain the case's view of the management perspective on ALM. What are the components of a typical ALM framework?

Method covered in: Unit 2 › Asset-Liability Management (ALM)

3. NEFT/RTGS Implementation (Solved)

Scenario: Traces the rollout of RTGS in India — first implemented by Standard Chartered Bank in the phased implementation of RTGS, with Standard Chartered Bank (SCB) and Saraswat Bank among the early adopters; the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) later became the receiving bank as the system enabled real-time, secure settlement between banks, reducing settlement risk in the banking sector.

Questions asked: What is RTGS? What are its advantages? What is Electronic Fund Transfer? What are its uses to business houses?

Method covered in: Unit 3 › Payment & Settlement Systems

4. Mutual Fund Launch Strategy (Solved, discussion-based)

Scenario: A fund house planning to launch a new mutual fund scheme (NFO) must decide on its distribution strategy — broker/distributor networks, cost-effectiveness, and how to reach and retain investors in a competitive retail market.

Questions asked: What are the distribution challenges discussed in the case? What alternatives does the case suggest to make the launch more cost-effective?

Method covered in: Unit 4 › Mutual Funds — Concept, Structure & Types

5. Lease vs. Loan-Purchase Decision (Solved)

Scenario: A company needs a machine/tractor for 5 years and must choose between leasing it or buying it using a 5-year bank loan at 14% interest, with equal annual instalments.

Best-effort figures from the book: Loan alternative — annual instalment of ₹1,00,000 for 5 years at 14% interest (interest and principal components computed year-by-year via an amortisation schedule). Lease alternative — annual lease payments discounted at an appropriate rate to find the Present Value of total lease payments, compared against the Present Value of the loan-purchase cash outflows.

Conclusion tested: The company should choose whichever alternative has the lower present value of total cash outflow — this is the standard lease-or-buy NPV comparison method.

Method covered in: Unit 4 › Leasing — Financial Evaluation

6. Merchant Banker's Capital-Structure Advice (Solved)

Scenario: A listed company (referred to as "Argus Ltd" in the book) trading near its 52-week low share price is deciding whether to raise fresh capital through equity (issuing new shares at the depressed price) or through debt, and approaches a merchant banker for advice.

Best-effort reasoning from the book: Issuing equity at a depressed price dilutes existing shareholders significantly for a given amount raised; raising debt instead avoids this dilution, provided the company can service the debt from its internal accruals/cash flow. The book's suggested reasons for preferring debt centre on avoiding excessive ownership dilution at a low share price.

Note: The specific rupee/share-count figures in this case were not transcribed with confidence — the reasoning and conclusion above are accurate, but check the book directly if you need the exact numbers.

Method covered in: Unit 5 › Merchant Banking — Functions & Services

7. IRDA Claim Dispute (Solved, discussion-based)

Scenario: A complaint lodged with IRDA against a life insurer, which repudiated a death claim on grounds of non-disclosure at the time of underwriting. The investigation found the insurer took more than 6 months from receiving the claim to deciding on its admissibility — longer than the permitted 1-year window for such investigations under the applicable norms.

Questions asked: What decision did IRDA take? Was the insurer (referred to as a "Life Insurance Co Ltd" in the book, similar to HDFC Standard Life) right in repudiating the claim?

Method covered in: Unit 5 › IRDA

Unsolved Case Study: Banking

Scenario: The Indian Financial System has undergone a sea change since the economic reforms of 1991. The vibrancy and rigour of a financial system, to a large extent, reflects and decides the economic growth of a country. Indian financial markets are not as hyperactive as Western financial markets, but are also not as premature as those in some other developing countries — regulators (mainly the RBI and SEBI) have achieved a fine balance between fostering growth and maintaining vigilance, which has helped Indian financial markets grow steadily without major meltdowns.

Questions (no solution given in the book — for self-practice):

  1. In the light of the above case, discuss the functions of the Reserve Bank of India in ensuring a sound banking system.
  2. Discuss the role of commercial banks in the economic development of our country.

Method covered in: Unit 1 › RBI & Key Banking Regulations and Unit 1 › Functions of Commercial Banks

Explain the different types of e-payment/transfer systems made available to retail customers by public sector banks in India. Are new-generation banks able to offer better payment/transfer services than Public Sector Banks? Give reasons.
On the "new-generation vs PSU banks" question, the book's model answer argues yes, for five reasons: (1) private/new-generation banks focus more on customer satisfaction and premium service; (2) they adopt productivity-enhancing technology (real-time information, efficient risk management) faster; (3) they pioneered direct selling agents who bring credit products to the customer's doorstep; (4) RBI has progressively allowed private banks to handle more government business, letting customers transact at any branch regardless of ownership; (5) they lead adoption of new technology — phone/ATM/mobile/SMS banking, online/internet/email banking, RTGS/NEFT/ECS/EFT, PIN-based smart/credit/debit cards, and travel cheques/offshore banking.
