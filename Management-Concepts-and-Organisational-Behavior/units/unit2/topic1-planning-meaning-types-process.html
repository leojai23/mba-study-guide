Unit 2Planning: Meaning, Scope, Process and Types

Book: Management Concepts and Organisational Behaviour (BA4102, Anna University MBA Sem I)

1. Overview

Planning is the first and most fundamental function of management. This topic covers its meaning, nature and scope, the steps every planning process follows, the types of plans an organisation uses, and planning's benefits and limitations.

2. Detailed Notes

1.1 Concept and Nature of Planning

Planning is the process of deciding in advance what is to be done, when it is to be done, how it is to be done, and who is to do it. It bridges where an organisation is now and where it wants to be in the future.

  • Intellectual Process: Planning requires thinking, forecasting and decision-making before action is taken.
  • Goal-Oriented: Every plan is prepared to achieve specific organisational objectives.
  • Pervasive: Planning is done at every level of management, though its scope and detail differ by level.
  • Continuous: Plans are regularly reviewed and revised as circumstances change.
  • Futuristic: Planning always looks ahead and deals with an uncertain future.
  • Flexible: A good plan should be capable of adjustment as conditions change.

1.2 Scope of Planning

Planning is pervasive — it is carried out at every level of the organisation (corporate, business and functional) and across every department (production, marketing, finance, personnel), though top management handles broader, long-range planning while lower levels handle narrower, short-range operational planning.

1.3 Objectives/Purpose of Planning

  • To provide direction and a clear sense of purpose to organisational activities.
  • To reduce the risks of uncertainty by anticipating change.
  • To minimise wasteful and overlapping activities by coordinating efforts in advance.
  • To facilitate control, since actual performance can only be measured against a plan.
  • To encourage innovative thinking, since planning forces managers to consider alternative courses of action.

1.4 Steps in the Planning Process

  1. Awareness of Opportunities: Identifying the opportunities and challenges that make planning necessary.
  2. Setting Objectives: Deciding what is to be achieved, for the organisation as a whole and for each sub-unit.
  3. Developing Premises: Establishing the assumptions (about the environment, economy, competition) on which the plan will be based.
  4. Identifying Alternatives: Searching for and listing the alternative courses of action available.
  5. Evaluating Alternatives: Weighing each alternative against the objectives and premises.
  6. Selecting an Alternative: Choosing the course of action to actually be adopted.
  7. Formulating Supporting Plans: Developing derivative plans to support the main plan.
  8. Implementing the Plan (Numberising): Converting plans into budgets and figures to make them meaningful for execution and control.

1.5 Types of Plans

TypeDescription
Objectives/GoalsThe end results an organisation seeks to achieve.
StrategiesBroad programmes of action for deploying resources to achieve objectives in a competitive environment.
PoliciesGeneral statements that guide thinking and decision-making within an area.
ProceduresChronological sequences of required actions for handling recurring activities.
RulesSpecific, definite requirements that leave no discretion (e.g. "No smoking").
ProgrammesA complex set of goals, policies, procedures and resource allocations needed to carry out a given course of action.
BudgetsA statement of expected results expressed in numerical (usually financial) terms.

Plans can also be classified by time horizon as Long-term (multi-year, strategic), Medium-term, and Short-term (operational, often annual) plans.

1.6 Importance of Planning

  • Gives direction to organisational effort and reduces aimless activity.
  • Reduces the impact of uncertainty and change by anticipating future conditions.
  • Minimises waste and redundancy by coordinating activities in advance.
  • Establishes the standards used for effective control.

1.7 Limitations of Planning

  • Planning is based on forecasts and assumptions about the future, which can turn out to be wrong.
  • It involves time and cost, which can be substantial for large organisations.
  • Rigid plans can reduce an organisation's ability to respond quickly to sudden change.
  • Internal resistance from employees who are comfortable with existing ways of working can limit a plan's effectiveness.

1.8 Essentials of Effective Planning

  • Plans should be clear, simple and based on well-defined objectives.
  • Plans should be flexible enough to accommodate change.
  • Plans should be practical and based on a realistic assessment of resources and the environment.
  • Plans should involve the people who will be responsible for carrying them out.

3. Key Terms

Planning
The process of deciding in advance what is to be done, when, how and by whom, to achieve a predetermined objective.
Policy
A general statement that guides thinking and decision-making within a particular area of the organisation.
Procedure
A chronological sequence of required actions for handling a recurring type of activity.
Budget
A plan expressed in numerical, usually financial, terms, stating expected results.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Planning = deciding in advance what, when, how, and by whom; intellectual, goal-oriented, pervasive, continuous, futuristic, flexible.
  • 8 steps in the planning process: Awareness of Opportunities, Setting Objectives, Developing Premises, Identifying Alternatives, Evaluating Alternatives, Selecting an Alternative, Formulating Supporting Plans, Numberising (budgeting).
  • Types of plans: Objectives, Strategies, Policies, Procedures, Rules, Programmes, Budgets — also classified by time horizon as long/medium/short-term.
  • Limitations: based on uncertain forecasts, costly in time/money, can create rigidity, may face internal resistance.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Define planning. Explain its nature.
    Show model answer
    Planning is the process of deciding in advance what is to be done, when, how and by whom. Its nature is: an intellectual process, goal-oriented, pervasive across all levels, continuous, futuristic, and flexible enough to adjust to change.
  2. Explain the steps involved in the planning process.
    Show model answer
    Awareness of opportunities, setting objectives, developing planning premises, identifying alternative courses of action, evaluating those alternatives, selecting the best one, formulating supporting (derivative) plans, and finally numberising the plan through budgets.
  3. Explain the various types of plans.
    Show model answer
    Objectives (end results sought), Strategies (broad resource-deployment programmes), Policies (guides to decision-making), Procedures (sequences for recurring activities), Rules (specific requirements with no discretion), Programmes (a complete set of goals/policies/procedures for a course of action), and Budgets (numerical statements of expected results).
  4. What are the limitations of planning?
    Show model answer
    Planning depends on forecasts and assumptions that can prove wrong; it takes significant time and cost; it can create rigidity that slows response to sudden change; and it may face internal resistance from employees comfortable with existing practices.
