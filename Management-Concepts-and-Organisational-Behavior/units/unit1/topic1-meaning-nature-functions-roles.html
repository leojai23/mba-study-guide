Unit 1Management: Meaning, Nature, Functions and Roles

Book: Management Concepts and Organisational Behaviour (BA4102, Anna University MBA Sem I)

1. Overview

This opening topic defines management itself — what it means, why it is considered both a science and an art, the levels at which it operates, and the functions and roles every manager performs regardless of level.

2. Detailed Notes

1.1 Meaning and Definition of Management

Management is the process of getting things done through and with people, by directing and coordinating the efforts of a group toward common goals, using the available resources effectively and efficiently.

ThinkerDefinition
Koontz and O'DonnelManagement is the creation and maintenance of an internal environment in an enterprise where individuals, working in groups, can perform efficiently and effectively toward the attainment of group goals.
James D. Mooney and Allan C. ReileyManagement is the art of directing and inspiring people.
Peter DruckerManagement is a multipurpose organ that manages a business, manages managers, and manages workers and work.
Louis AllenManagement is what a manager does.

1.2 Nature of Management

Management can be understood through the following characteristics:

  1. Management is Universal: It applies to every kind of organisation — business, government, charitable or religious — wherever people work together toward a goal.
  2. Management is a Human Activity: It directs and coordinates the human and material resources of an organisation; without people, there is nothing to manage.
  3. Management is Goal-Oriented: Every managerial activity is carried out to achieve predetermined objectives.
  4. Management is a Continuous Process: It is a never-ending series of functions — planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling — performed continuously, not once and for all.
  5. Management is a Career: It offers interesting, stimulating and lucrative career opportunities across functions like marketing, finance, HR, and operations.
  6. Management Denotes Authority: Authority and responsibility flow through every level of the organisational hierarchy, from top to bottom.
  7. Management is Dynamic: It must adapt to a constantly changing environment — markets, technology and people all change, and management adapts with them.
  8. Management is Primarily a Discipline/Profession: It requires specialised knowledge and training, though it is still evolving toward the formal status of a full profession.
  9. Management is a Science as well as an Art: It has a systematic body of knowledge (science) but also requires skilled, situational judgement in application (art).

1.3 Scope of Management

Management as a discipline covers a wide range of activities and practices, broadly grouped under the functional areas of a business:

  • Production Management: Planning, organising and controlling production activities, machines and processes.
  • Marketing Management: Deals with marketing mix components — product, pricing, promotion, warehousing, distribution channels, etc.
  • Financial Management: Involves financial planning, budgeting, costing and statistical control.
  • Personnel Management: Recruiting, training and developing employees, keeping records, and maintaining social welfare.
  • Purchasing Management: Procurement of materials — inviting tenders, selecting vendors, negotiating and terminating contracts.
  • Maintenance Management: Inspection and upkeep of plant, buildings and equipment.
  • Office Management: Office layout, staffing, equipment and information flow.
  • Development Management: Research and development to improve products, processes and methods.
  • Management as an Inter-Disciplinary Approach: Management principles draw on economics, psychology, sociology and mathematics, making it a genuinely inter-disciplinary field of study and practice.

1.4 Functions of Management

Henri Fayol identified five core functions that together form the management process:

  1. Planning: Deciding in advance what is to be done, when, how and by whom — preparing objectives and the course of action to achieve them.
  2. Organising: Arranging resources (people, materials, equipment) and establishing authority relationships so the plan can be carried out efficiently.
  3. Staffing: Filling and keeping filled the positions in the organisation structure through recruitment, selection, training and development.
  4. Directing: Guiding, motivating and supervising subordinates so they work willingly and effectively toward organisational goals.
  5. Controlling: Measuring actual performance against planned performance and taking corrective action where deviations are found.

Beyond these five, management also performs supporting functional activities: Financial Management, Purchasing Management, Personnel Management, Production Management, Maintenance Management, and Office Management.

1.5 Managerial Roles (Henry Mintzberg)

Mintzberg identified ten roles every manager plays, grouped into three categories:

CategoryRoles
Interpersonal RolesFigurehead — performs ceremonial/symbolic duties; Leader — motivates and directs subordinates; Liaison — maintains a network of outside contacts.
Informational RolesMonitor — collects information from inside and outside the organisation; Disseminator — shares information with subordinates; Spokesperson — shares information outside the organisation.
Decisional RolesEntrepreneur — initiates change and new projects; Disturbance Handler — resolves conflicts and unexpected problems; Resource Allocator — decides how resources are distributed; Negotiator — represents the organisation in negotiations.

1.6 Levels of Management

LevelWhoPrimary Concern
Top ManagementBoard of Directors, CEO, General ManagersSetting overall objectives, policies and long-term direction — conceptual (thinking) skills matter most here.
Middle ManagementDepartmental/Divisional HeadsTranslating top management's plans into departmental goals and coordinating lower-level work — needs a balance of all three skill types.
Lower/Supervisory ManagementForemen, Supervisors, Section OfficersDirectly overseeing day-to-day operational work of the workforce — technical skills matter most here.

1.7 Management as an Art, Science and Profession

  • As a Science: Management has a systematic body of knowledge, principles and techniques that can be taught and applied, and its outcomes can be tested and verified — though, unlike physical sciences, it deals with unpredictable human behaviour, so it is better described as a "soft" or social science.
  • As an Art: Applying management knowledge skilfully to a specific, real-world situation requires creativity, judgement and personal skill — which is why two managers with the same knowledge can still get different results.
  • As a Profession: Management is moving toward professional status — it requires specialised knowledge, formal education, and (increasingly) adherence to a code of conduct — though it still lacks the universal licensing/certification requirements of established professions like law or medicine.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Management = getting things done through people, directing/coordinating group effort toward common goals.
  • Nine traits of management's nature: Universal, Human Activity, Goal-Oriented, Continuous Process, Career, Denotes Authority, Dynamic, Discipline/Profession, Science as well as Art.
  • Fayol's 5 functions: Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Controlling.
  • Mintzberg's 10 roles in 3 groups: Interpersonal (Figurehead, Leader, Liaison); Informational (Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson); Decisional (Entrepreneur, Disturbance Handler, Resource Allocator, Negotiator).
  • 3 levels of management: Top (conceptual skill), Middle (balanced skills), Lower/Supervisory (technical skill).
  • Katz's 3 managerial skills: Technical, Human, Conceptual — human skill matters equally at every level.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Define management. State any two definitions given by different thinkers.
    Show model answer
    Management is the process of getting things done through and with people, directing and coordinating group effort toward common goals. Koontz and O'Donnel define it as the creation and maintenance of an internal environment where people working in groups can perform efficiently toward group goals; Peter Drucker calls it a multipurpose organ that manages a business, manages managers, and manages workers and work.
  2. Explain the nature of management.
    Show model answer
    Management is universal (applies to every kind of organisation), a human activity, goal-oriented, a continuous process, offers career opportunities, denotes authority flowing through the hierarchy, is dynamic (adapts to change), is primarily a discipline/profession, and is both a science (systematic body of knowledge) and an art (skilled application).
  3. Explain the functions of management as given by Henri Fayol.
    Show model answer
    Planning (deciding in advance what to do), Organising (arranging resources and authority relationships), Staffing (filling positions through recruitment and training), Directing (guiding and motivating subordinates), and Controlling (measuring performance against plans and correcting deviations).
  4. Explain Mintzberg's managerial roles.
    Show model answer
    Ten roles in three categories: Interpersonal (Figurehead, Leader, Liaison), Informational (Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson), and Decisional (Entrepreneur, Disturbance Handler, Resource Allocator, Negotiator).
  5. Describe the levels of management and the skills required at each.
    Show model answer
    Top management sets overall objectives and needs conceptual skill; Middle management translates plans into departmental goals and needs a balance of technical, human and conceptual skill; Lower/Supervisory management oversees day-to-day operational work and needs technical skill most. Human skill matters equally at every level.
  6. Is management a science, an art, or a profession? Explain.
    Show model answer
    Management has elements of all three: it is a science because it has a systematic, testable body of knowledge; an art because applying that knowledge to a specific situation requires skill and judgement; and it is moving toward a profession because it increasingly requires specialised education, though it still lacks the universal licensing of established professions.
