Unit 3Motivation Theories and Job Performance

Book: Management Concepts and Organisational Behaviour (BA4102, Anna University MBA Sem I)

1. Overview

Motivation explains why employees put in effort at work. This topic covers the meaning and elements of motivation, the major content and process theories, and how motivation links to job performance.

2. Detailed Notes

5.1 Meaning and Definition of Motivation

Motivation is the set of forces that initiate, direct and sustain effort toward achieving a goal. At work, it is what drives an employee to put in effort toward organisational objectives.

5.2 Nature and Elements of Motivation

  • Intensity: How much effort a person puts in.
  • Direction: Whether that effort is channelled toward the organisation's goals.
  • Persistence: How long a person sustains that effort.

Motivation is internal to the individual (it cannot be directly observed, only inferred from behaviour), goal-directed, and varies in strength both between people and within the same person over time.

5.3 Importance of Motivation

  • Higher motivation is generally associated with higher productivity and quality of work.
  • Improves employee satisfaction, reducing absenteeism and turnover.
  • Encourages initiative, creativity and willingness to go beyond the minimum required.

5.4 Content Theories of Motivation

TheoryCore Idea
Maslow's Need Hierarchy TheoryNeeds are arranged in a hierarchy — Physiological, Safety, Social, Esteem, Self-Actualisation — and a satisfied lower need stops motivating, pushing the person toward the next level up.
Herzberg's Two-Factor TheoryDistinguishes Hygiene Factors (pay, working conditions — prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate) from Motivators (achievement, recognition, growth — create genuine satisfaction and motivation).
McClelland's Needs TheoryPeople are driven by three learned needs in varying degrees: Need for Achievement (nAch), Need for Power (nPow), and Need for Affiliation (nAff).
Alderfer's ERG TheoryCondenses Maslow's hierarchy into three needs — Existence, Relatedness, Growth — and allows more than one need to be active at once, unlike Maslow's strict hierarchy.

5.5 Process Theories of Motivation

TheoryCore Idea
McGregor's Theory X and Theory YTheory X assumes employees are inherently lazy and need close control; Theory Y assumes employees are self-motivated and seek responsibility — a manager's assumptions shape their management style.
Vroom's Expectancy TheoryMotivation depends on three beliefs multiplied together: Expectancy (effort leads to performance), Instrumentality (performance leads to reward), and Valence (the reward is valued).
Porter and Lawler ModelExtends expectancy theory, showing that performance leads to both intrinsic and extrinsic rewards, which, filtered through perceived fairness, determine satisfaction.

5.6 Motivation and Job Performance

Job performance is generally understood as a function of Ability × Motivation × Opportunity — even a highly skilled employee will underperform if they lack motivation, and even a highly motivated employee needs the right ability and opportunity to translate effort into results. This is why management practice pairs motivation theory with training (building ability) and well-designed jobs and resources (providing opportunity).

3. Key Terms

Motivation
The set of internal forces that initiate, direct and sustain effort toward achieving a goal.
Maslow's Need Hierarchy
A theory arranging human needs in five levels — Physiological, Safety, Social, Esteem, Self-Actualisation — with satisfied lower needs ceasing to motivate.
Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory
A motivation theory distinguishing Hygiene Factors (prevent dissatisfaction) from Motivators (create genuine satisfaction).
Vroom's Expectancy Theory
A theory holding that motivation depends on Expectancy, Instrumentality and Valence multiplied together.
Theory X and Theory Y
McGregor's contrasting sets of managerial assumptions about employees — inherently lazy (X) versus self-motivated (Y).

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Motivation = internal forces that initiate, direct and sustain effort; elements: Intensity, Direction, Persistence.
  • Content theories: Maslow's Need Hierarchy, Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, McClelland's Needs Theory (nAch/nPow/nAff), Alderfer's ERG Theory.
  • Process theories: McGregor's Theory X/Y, Vroom's Expectancy Theory, Porter and Lawler Model.
  • Job Performance = f(Ability x Motivation x Opportunity).

5. Practice Questions

  1. Define motivation. Explain its elements.
    Show model answer
    Motivation is the set of internal forces that initiate, direct and sustain effort toward a goal. Its elements are Intensity (how much effort), Direction (whether effort targets the right goals), and Persistence (how long the effort is sustained).
  2. Explain Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory.
    Show model answer
    Maslow arranged human needs into five levels — Physiological, Safety, Social, Esteem, and Self-Actualisation. Once a lower-level need is satisfied, it stops motivating behaviour, and the individual is driven to satisfy the next level up.
  3. Explain Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory.
    Show model answer
    Herzberg distinguished Hygiene Factors (pay, working conditions, policies) which prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate, from Motivators (achievement, recognition, growth) which create genuine satisfaction and drive motivation.
  4. Explain Vroom's Expectancy Theory.
    Show model answer
    Motivation is the product of three beliefs: Expectancy (that effort will lead to performance), Instrumentality (that performance will lead to a reward), and Valence (that the reward is personally valued). If any factor is zero, motivation is zero.
  5. How is job performance related to motivation?
    Show model answer
    Job performance is commonly expressed as a function of Ability, Motivation, and Opportunity (Performance = f(Ability x Motivation x Opportunity)) — a skilled but unmotivated employee, or a motivated but unskilled one, will both underperform, showing that all three elements must be present together.
