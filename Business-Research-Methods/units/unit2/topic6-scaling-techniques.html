Unit 2Scaling Techniques

Book: Business Research Methods (BA4205, Anna University MBA Sem II, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

Scaling converts qualitative judgements into quantitative data that can be analysed statistically. This topic covers the types of scales and the main scaling techniques used in business research.

2. Detailed Notes

6.1 Introduction to Scaling

Scaling is the process of assigning numbers to an object or respondent's attitude, opinion or perception so that it can be compared, ranked or statistically analysed against others.

6.2 Process of Scale Construction

  1. Define the attitude or construct to be scaled.
  2. Generate a pool of potential items/statements.
  3. Select the scaling technique to apply.
  4. Pre-test the scale on a sample.
  5. Refine and finalise the scale based on pre-test results.

6.3 Types of Scales (Levels of Measurement)

Scale TypeDescription
Nominal ScaleLabels/categorises data with no inherent order (e.g. gender, brand names). Only counts/frequencies are meaningful.
Ordinal ScaleRanks data in order, but the gaps between ranks are not necessarily equal (e.g. satisfaction ranked "low/medium/high").
Interval ScaleRanked data with equal intervals between points, but no true/absolute zero (e.g. temperature in °C).
Ratio ScaleInterval scale with a true zero point, allowing meaningful ratios (e.g. sales revenue, age, income).

6.4 Scaling Techniques

Ranking Scales (Comparative)

Respondents directly compare items against each other and arrange them in order (e.g. rank 5 brands from most to least preferred).

Rating Scales (Non-Comparative)

Respondents evaluate each item independently against a fixed scale (e.g. a 5-point Likert scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree").

6.5 Validity and Reliability of the Scaling Instrument

As with any measurement tool, a scale must be checked for validity (does it truly capture the intended attitude?) and reliability (does it give consistent results when repeated?) before its results can be trusted.

6.6 Importance of Scaling

Scaling allows subjective concepts such as attitudes, satisfaction and preference to be expressed numerically, enabling statistical comparison, ranking and hypothesis testing that would otherwise be impossible with purely qualitative descriptions.

6.7 Issues in Designing Attitude Scales

  • Choosing the appropriate number of scale points (too few loses precision; too many can confuse respondents).
  • Avoiding leading, ambiguous or double-barrelled statements.
  • Deciding whether to include a neutral midpoint.
  • Ensuring the scale is balanced, with an equal number of favourable and unfavourable positions.

3. Key Terms

Nominal Scale
A scale that labels or categorises data with no inherent order.
Ordinal Scale
A scale that ranks data in order without equal gaps between ranks.
Interval Scale
A ranked scale with equal intervals between points but no true zero.
Ratio Scale
An interval scale with a true zero point, allowing meaningful ratios.
Likert Scale
A common rating scale on which respondents indicate their degree of agreement with a statement.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Scale construction: define construct → generate items → choose technique → pre-test → refine.
  • Four levels of measurement: nominal (labels) < ordinal (ranks) < interval (equal gaps, no true zero) < ratio (true zero).
  • Ranking scales = comparative (items compared to each other); Rating scales = non-comparative (items rated independently).
  • Scale must have validity (measures the right thing) and reliability (consistent results).
  • Design issues: number of scale points, avoiding leading/ambiguous items, neutral midpoint, balance.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Explain the four levels of measurement scales with examples.
    Show model answer
    Nominal scales label data with no order, such as brand names. Ordinal scales rank data without equal gaps, such as low/medium/high satisfaction. Interval scales have equal intervals but no true zero, such as temperature in Celsius. Ratio scales have a true zero and allow meaningful ratios, such as sales revenue or age.
  2. Differentiate between ranking and rating scales.
    Show model answer
    Ranking (comparative) scales require respondents to directly compare items against each other and arrange them in order, such as ranking five brands by preference. Rating (non-comparative) scales ask respondents to evaluate each item independently against a fixed scale, such as a 5-point Likert agreement scale.
  3. What issues must be considered when designing an attitude scale?
    Show model answer
    Key issues include choosing an appropriate number of scale points, avoiding leading, ambiguous or double-barrelled statements, deciding whether to include a neutral midpoint, and ensuring the scale is balanced with an equal number of favourable and unfavourable positions.
