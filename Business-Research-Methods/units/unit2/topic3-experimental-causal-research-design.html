Unit 2Experimental (Causal) Research Design

Book: Business Research Methods (BA4205, Anna University MBA Sem II, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

Experimental (causal) research design is used to establish cause-and-effect relationships. This topic covers the logic of causality, the main experimental design types, and how the validity of experimental findings is assessed.

2. Detailed Notes

3.1 Introduction

Experimental research deliberately manipulates one or more independent variables under controlled conditions to observe the effect on a dependent variable, allowing the researcher to draw causal conclusions that descriptive research cannot.

3.2 Criteria for Causality in Experimental Research

  • Concomitant variation — the cause and effect must vary together as predicted.
  • Time order of occurrence — the cause must occur before (or simultaneously with) the effect.
  • Elimination of other possible causes — other plausible explanations for the effect must be ruled out.

3.3 Causal Relationships and Basic Principles of Experimental Design

A true experiment is built on three basic principles: manipulation of the independent variable by the researcher, control of extraneous factors that could also explain the effect, and randomisation of subjects to groups to spread out the influence of unknown factors evenly.

Treatment and Control Group

The treatment (experimental) group receives the manipulated independent variable (e.g. a new advertising campaign); the control group does not, providing a baseline against which the effect of the treatment can be measured.

3.4 Types/Methods of Experimental Research Design

TypeDescription
Pre-Experimental DesignsSimplest designs, with little or no control over extraneous variables or randomisation (e.g. a one-group, before-after study); weak for establishing causality.
True Experimental DesignsUse random assignment to treatment and control groups with proper controls (e.g. pretest-posttest control group design), giving the strongest basis for causal conclusions.
Quasi-Experimental DesignsUsed when full randomisation is not possible (e.g. in field settings); some control is exercised but groups are not fully randomised.
Statistical DesignsUse statistical techniques (e.g. factorial designs) to control for multiple independent variables and their interactions simultaneously.

3.5 Validity of Findings

  • Internal Validity — the degree to which the observed effect can confidently be attributed to the manipulated variable and not to some other factor.
  • External Validity — the degree to which the findings can be generalised beyond the specific experimental setting to other people, places and times.
  • Trade-offs Between Internal and External Validity — tightly controlled lab experiments tend to have high internal but lower external validity, while realistic field experiments tend to have the reverse.

3.6 Application, Significance and Limitations

Experimental design is applied in test marketing, product testing and advertising-effectiveness studies. It is significant because it is the strongest design for establishing causality. Its limitations include high cost, the difficulty of controlling all extraneous variables (especially in field settings), and possible artificiality when conducted in a laboratory.

3. Key Terms

Experimental Research
Research that deliberately manipulates an independent variable under controlled conditions to observe its effect on a dependent variable.
Treatment Group
The group in an experiment that receives the manipulated independent variable.
Control Group
The group in an experiment that does not receive the treatment, serving as a baseline.
Internal Validity
The degree to which an observed effect can be confidently attributed to the manipulated variable rather than another factor.
External Validity
The degree to which experimental findings can be generalised beyond the specific setting studied.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Causality criteria: concomitant variation, correct time order, elimination of other causes.
  • Experimental design principles: manipulation, control, randomisation.
  • Design types: pre-experimental (weak) < quasi-experimental < true experimental (strongest, randomised).
  • Internal validity = confidence effect is due to the treatment; external validity = generalisability.
  • Trade-off: lab experiments favour internal validity, field experiments favour external validity.

5. Practice Questions

  1. What criteria must be met to establish a causal relationship?
    Show model answer
    Three criteria must be met: concomitant variation (the cause and effect vary together as predicted), correct time order (the cause precedes the effect), and elimination of other plausible causes for the observed effect.
  2. Distinguish between true experimental and quasi-experimental designs.
    Show model answer
    True experimental designs randomly assign subjects to treatment and control groups under full researcher control, giving the strongest basis for causal conclusions. Quasi-experimental designs are used when full randomisation is not feasible, such as in real-world field settings, and provide weaker but still useful evidence of causality.
  3. Explain internal and external validity and the trade-off between them.
    Show model answer
    Internal validity is the degree to which an observed effect can be confidently attributed to the manipulated variable rather than some other factor, while external validity is the degree to which findings generalise beyond the specific experimental setting. Tightly controlled lab experiments tend to have high internal validity but lower external validity, while realistic field experiments tend to show the opposite trade-off.
