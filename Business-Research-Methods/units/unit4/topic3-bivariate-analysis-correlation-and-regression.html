Unit 4Bi-variate Analysis: Correlation and Regression

Book: Business Research Methods (BA4205, Anna University MBA Sem II, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

This topic covers the two most common bi-variate statistical techniques: correlation, which measures the strength and direction of a relationship, and regression, which models that relationship so one variable can be predicted from another.

2. Detailed Notes

3.1 Cross Tabulation and Use of Percentages

Cross tabulation displays the joint distribution of two variables in a grid, making it easy to see how categories of one variable relate to categories of another. Converting cell counts to percentages (row-wise, column-wise, or of the grand total) makes patterns easier to compare across groups of different sizes.

3.2 Correlation Analysis

Meaning

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables, expressed as a coefficient between −1 and +1.

Types of Correlation

  • Positive correlation — both variables increase or decrease together.
  • Negative correlation — one variable increases as the other decreases.
  • Zero/no correlation — no consistent linear relationship between the variables.

Scatter Diagram

A scatter diagram plots paired observations of the two variables on a graph, giving a visual first impression of whether a relationship exists and its approximate direction and strength.

Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Correlation

The most widely used correlation measure for quantitative data, calculated as:

r = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ √[Σ(x − x̄)2 · Σ(y − ȳ)2]

r ranges from −1 (perfect negative) to +1 (perfect positive), with 0 indicating no linear relationship.

Spearman's Rank Correlation

Used for ranked (ordinal) data or when a non-parametric measure is preferred:

ρ = 1 − [6Σd2 ÷ n(n2 − 1)]

where d is the difference between the ranks of each pair of observations and n is the number of pairs.

3.3 Regression Analysis

Meaning of Regression

Regression analysis models the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables, allowing the dependent variable's value to be estimated/predicted.

Regression Coefficient

For the simple linear regression equation Y = a + bX, b is the regression coefficient, representing the change in Y for a one-unit change in X.

Two Lines of Regression

There are two regression lines — Y on X (predicting Y from X) and X on Y (predicting X from Y) — which coincide only when correlation is perfect (r = ±1).

Relationship between Correlation and Regression

Correlation measures the degree of association between two variables, while regression goes further to express that relationship as an equation usable for prediction. The correlation coefficient is in fact the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients (byx and bxy).

Application and Difference

Correlation is used simply to establish whether and how strongly two variables move together; regression is used when the goal is to predict or explain one variable's value using another.

3. Key Terms

Correlation
A statistical measure of the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables.
Karl Pearson's Coefficient (r)
A correlation coefficient ranging from -1 to +1 measuring the linear relationship between two quantitative variables.
Spearman's Rank Correlation
A non-parametric correlation measure used for ranked (ordinal) data.
Regression Analysis
A technique that models the relationship between a dependent and one or more independent variables for prediction.
Regression Coefficient
In Y = a + bX, the value b representing the change in Y for a one-unit change in X.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Correlation types: positive, negative, zero/no correlation; r ranges from -1 to +1.
  • Pearson's r = Sigma(x-xbar)(y-ybar) / sqrt[Sigma(x-xbar)^2 . Sigma(y-ybar)^2].
  • Spearman's rank correlation: rho = 1 - [6*Sigma(d^2) / n(n^2-1)], for ranked data.
  • Regression equation: Y = a + bX; b = regression coefficient (change in Y per unit change in X).
  • Correlation measures association strength; regression models the relationship for prediction. r = geometric mean of byx and bxy.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Define correlation and explain its types.
    Show model answer
    Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables. It can be positive (both variables move together), negative (one increases as the other decreases), or zero/no correlation (no consistent linear relationship).
  2. State Karl Pearson's formula for the coefficient of correlation and explain its range.
    Show model answer
    Karl Pearson's coefficient of correlation is r = Sigma(x-xbar)(y-ybar) / sqrt[Sigma(x-xbar)^2 . Sigma(y-ybar)^2]. It ranges from -1 (perfect negative correlation) to +1 (perfect positive correlation), with 0 indicating no linear relationship.
  3. Differentiate between correlation and regression analysis.
    Show model answer
    Correlation measures the degree and direction of association between two variables without implying prediction. Regression goes further, expressing the relationship as an equation (Y = a + bX) that allows the value of the dependent variable to be predicted from the independent variable; the correlation coefficient is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients.
  4. What are the two lines of regression? When do they coincide?
    Show model answer
    The two lines of regression are the regression of Y on X (used to predict Y from X) and the regression of X on Y (used to predict X from Y). These two lines coincide only when the correlation between the variables is perfect, i.e. r = +1 or r = -1.
