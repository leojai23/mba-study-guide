Unit 3Observation and Survey Method

Book: Business Research Methods (BA4205, Anna University MBA Sem II, Regulation 2021)

1. Overview

Beyond questionnaires, researchers also gather primary data by directly observing behaviour or by conducting structured surveys. This topic compares these two closely related methods.

2. Detailed Notes

3.1 Observation

Introduction

Observation involves systematically watching and recording behaviour, events or conditions as they naturally occur, without relying on respondents to report on themselves.

Types of Observation

TypeDescription
Participant ObservationThe observer becomes part of the group or situation being studied.
Non-Participant ObservationThe observer remains separate, watching without taking part.
Structured ObservationUses a predefined checklist/format specifying exactly what to record.
Unstructured ObservationOpen-ended recording of whatever seems relevant, common in exploratory studies.

Process

Define what behaviour to observe → select the setting and observation method → train observers → record observations systematically → analyse the recorded data.

Significance and Limitations

Observation captures actual behaviour rather than reported behaviour, avoiding recall and social-desirability bias. However, it cannot reveal motives, attitudes or intentions behind the behaviour, can be time-consuming, and the presence of an observer may itself alter behaviour.

3.2 Survey

Introduction

A survey is a method of collecting data from a sample of respondents, typically through questionnaires or interviews, to describe the characteristics or opinions of a larger population.

Methods

Personal (face-to-face) surveys, telephone surveys, mail surveys, and online/electronic surveys.

Significance and Limitations

Surveys can efficiently cover large, geographically dispersed samples and produce data that can be statistically generalised. Limitations include the risk of non-response, respondent dishonesty or inaccurate recall, and the cost of reaching a sufficiently large, representative sample.

3.3 Survey versus Observation

AspectSurveyObservation
Data sourceRespondent self-reportDirectly observed behaviour
Can capture attitudes/motives?YesNo (only behaviour)
Risk of reporting biasHigherLower
Typical scaleLarge samplesOften smaller, intensive

3. Key Terms

Observation
A data collection method involving systematically watching and recording behaviour as it naturally occurs.
Participant Observation
Observation in which the observer becomes part of the group or situation being studied.
Survey
A method of collecting data from a sample of respondents, typically via questionnaire or interview, to describe a larger population.

4. Exam-Prep Summary

  • Observation types: participant vs non-participant; structured vs unstructured.
  • Observation captures actual behaviour, avoids recall bias, but cannot reveal motives/attitudes.
  • Survey methods: personal, telephone, mail, online.
  • Survey vs Observation: self-report vs directly observed; surveys can capture attitudes, observation cannot.

5. Practice Questions

  1. Differentiate between participant and non-participant observation.
    Show model answer
    In participant observation, the observer becomes part of the group or situation being studied, gaining an insider's perspective. In non-participant observation, the observer remains separate from the group, watching and recording behaviour without taking part in it.
  2. What are the significance and limitations of the observation method?
    Show model answer
    Observation captures actual behaviour rather than self-reported behaviour, avoiding recall and social-desirability bias. However, it cannot reveal the underlying motives, attitudes or intentions behind the behaviour, can be time-consuming, and the presence of an observer may itself alter the behaviour being studied.
  3. Compare survey and observation as methods of data collection.
    Show model answer
    A survey collects data through respondent self-report via questionnaires or interviews and can capture attitudes and opinions, but carries a higher risk of reporting bias. Observation directly records actual behaviour with lower reporting bias, but cannot capture underlying motives or attitudes and is often conducted on a smaller, more intensive scale.
